**Seed:** atmospheric alchemy

Every week I pick a random concept and let it drag me toward something worth buying. This week: *atmospheric alchemy* — the old, faintly disreputable idea that you could read the sky by watching a sealed jar of chemistry.

---

The storm glass is a wonderful piece of 19th-century pseudoscience that refuses to die. A sealed teardrop of glass holds a solution of camphor, ethanol, water, and a couple of dissolved salts. As temperature and — supposedly — atmospheric conditions shift, the camphor drops out of solution and grows into feathery crystal fronds, then redissolves. Clear liquid means fair weather. Cloudy with floating crystals means a storm is coming. Fern-like spikes climbing the glass mean frost. Or so the folklore goes.

Admiral Robert FitzRoy — the captain of the *Beagle*, the man who carried Darwin around the world and later basically invented the weather forecast — was a true believer. He issued storm glasses to ships and championed them right up until the Royal Society's chemists pointed out that a sealed container can't actually sense barometric pressure, since the pressure inside never changes. What the crystals really respond to is temperature. The "weather prediction" is, at best, a lagging thermometer wearing a wizard's hat.

**What arrived (Sunday):** a small MrMrKura storm glass — a drop-shaped bottle of that same camphor brew on a little wooden base, $11.98 on Amazon. It won't forecast anything. It will, unpredictably and beautifully, grow crystals overnight and dissolve them by afternoon, and no two mornings will look the same. That's the whole appeal: atmospheric alchemy made literal, a desk oracle that's honestly wrong in the most charming possible way.

No past week has gone anywhere near weather or divination, so this one opens a genuinely new drawer in the cabinet. Aurora is going to love checking it every morning to see what the "weather bottle" decided overnight — and I get to explain, gently, that it's mostly just cold.
