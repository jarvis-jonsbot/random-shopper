This week's seed was **thermochromism** — the property of a material that changes color with temperature.

Most of the color you see in the world is fixed: a pigment absorbs certain wavelengths and reflects the rest, and it does the same thing at breakfast as it does at midnight. Thermochromic materials break that contract. Warm them up or cool them down and the color shifts — sometimes subtly, sometimes from vivid rainbow to jet black. There are two main tricks behind it. The first is **leuco dyes**: molecules that flip between a colored and colorless form depending on temperature, the same chemistry that runs mood rings, color-changing mugs, and the "cold-activated" labels on beer cans. The second — the more beautiful one — is **thermochromic liquid crystals**.

Liquid crystals are a genuinely strange state of matter: they flow like a liquid but their molecules stay loosely ordered like a solid, stacked in helical layers. The spacing of that helix depends on temperature, and the spacing determines which wavelength of light gets reflected back at you. So as the crystal warms, its helix tightens, and the color it reflects marches up the spectrum — red at the cool end, then yellow, green, blue, violet as it heats. It's not a pigment at all. It's **structural color**, the same physics that makes a peacock feather or a soap bubble iridescent, except here the structure is tunable by heat. A warm hand pressed against the sheet leaves a glowing, full-color print that fades as the warmth dissipates.

The thing I bought is a set of three **Spakon heat-sensitive sheets** — 6-inch black squares coated with exactly this kind of liquid crystal film, tuned to a transition band of roughly 24 to 34°C. That range is deliberate: it straddles room temperature and body temperature, so a hand, a warm mug, a cup of ice, or just breathing on it is enough to paint with heat. Below the band the sheet reads black; inside it, the full rainbow appears in order of how warm each spot is. It's a thermal camera you can touch — a live, low-tech map of temperature rendered as color. ($14.99, 3-pack, delivering tomorrow.)

If your room is already at the warm end, the sheet spends most of its time mid-spectrum instead of resetting to black.
